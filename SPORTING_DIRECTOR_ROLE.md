# The Sporting Director: Role Documentation

Source vision document for this project. `VISION.md` and `DATA_REQUIREMENTS.md` track progress and data needs against the sections below — their `§` references point here.

## 1. Why the role exists

For most of football's history one man — the manager — picked the team, ran training, chose signings, negotiated fees and often controlled the youth setup. That model has two structural weaknesses:

1. **Time horizon mismatch.** The average managerial tenure in the top five leagues is roughly 12–18 months. Squad building takes three to five years. A manager optimising for his own survival will always trade future value for present points.
2. **Skill mismatch.** Coaching a training session and negotiating a €40m instalment structure with an agent are unrelated professional skills. Very few people are excellent at both.

The Sporting Director (variously Director of Football, Technical Director, Head of Football Operations, *Sportdirektor*) exists to hold the long-horizon, non-coaching half of that job. The core principle is **institutional continuity**: the club's identity, recruitment model and squad plan should survive the departure of any one head coach.

The trade-off is that you introduce a seam into the organisation. Every failure mode of the role is a failure at that seam.

## 2. Objectives

A well-specified mandate usually has five pillars.

### 2.1 Build and maintain a squad capable of the club's competitive objective
Not "sign good players." Sign the *right shape* of squad: correct age curve, correct positional depth, correct balance between proven and developing, correct number of players in each contract band. The squad is a portfolio, not a shopping list.

### 2.2 Protect and grow the value of the playing asset base
For all but a handful of clubs, transfer trading is the largest single lever on the P&L. The Sporting Director owns the buy-low/sell-high cycle: buying inside the value window (typically 20–24), selling before the depreciation curve bites (typically 27+), and never letting a saleable asset run into the final year of a contract by accident.

### 2.3 Keep the club solvent and compliant
Wage bill as a percentage of turnover, amortisation load, PSR/FFP headroom, homegrown and squad registration rules. These are hard constraints that the head coach should never have to think about — but which quietly determine what he is allowed to ask for.

### 2.4 Define and defend the playing identity
The club decides how it wants to play *before* it hires the person who will coach it. This is the most contested pillar and the one most often fudged. Done well, it means the recruitment model and the academy curriculum point in the same direction as the first team. Done badly, it becomes a straitjacket that eliminates good coaches for stylistic reasons.

### 2.5 Own coach succession
Maintain a live, ranked shortlist of head coaches whose methods fit the identity, so that a sacking is a decision rather than a panic. A Sporting Director who has to start the search on the day of the dismissal has already failed at the job.

## 3. Where the role sits

Typical reporting line:

```
Board / Ownership
        |
   CEO
        |
Sporting Director ──────────────┐
   |        |        |          |
Head of  Head of  Head of    Head Coach
Recruit-  Medical  Academy    (first team)
ment                              |
   |                          Coaching staff
Scouts,                       Analysts
Data team                     Sports science
```

Two variants matter:

- **Head coach reports to Sporting Director** (most European clubs, Brighton, Brentford, City). The Sporting Director is the head coach's line manager. Clean, but requires the SD to have genuine football credibility or the coach will not respect it.
- **Head coach and Sporting Director both report to CEO** (common in England, and at Liverpool under Edwards). Peers rather than boss and subordinate. Reduces the risk of an SD overreaching, but pushes conflict resolution up to a non-football executive.

The single most important structural question: **who has the final word on a signing?** There is no universally correct answer, but there is a universally correct requirement — it must be written down and everyone must know it before the first disagreement, not after.

## 4. Working with the head coach

### 4.1 The division of labour

| Domain | Head Coach | Sporting Director |
|---|---|---|
| Tactics, selection, training | Owns | No input |
| Squad needs (what profile, where) | Defines the need | Challenges and validates it |
| Identifying candidates | Advises | Owns |
| Valuation, negotiation, contract terms | No involvement | Owns |
| Final approval of signing | Veto or joint sign-off | Recommends |
| Loan and sale decisions | Consulted | Owns |
| Academy promotion | Decides who plays | Owns the pathway |
| Contract renewals | Consulted | Owns |
| Medical, sports science, analysis staffing | Consulted | Owns |

The clean formulation: **the coach specifies, the director sources.** The coach says "I need a left-footed centre back who can defend a high line and break lines with his passing, and I need him for the next two seasons." The director's job is to convert that into a profile, run it against the scouting and data pipeline, produce a ranked list, model the cost, and execute.

Crucially, the coach should not be presenting names. The moment a coach arrives with a name — usually via an agent — the process has been inverted.

### 4.2 Working rhythm

- **Weekly**: 30–60 minutes, one-to-one, standing. Squad status, injuries, minutes distribution, anything drifting.
- **Monthly**: squad review with the analysis lead. Who is over- or under-playing relative to plan, who is stagnating, which academy player is ready.
- **Quarterly / pre-window**: formal window planning session. Priorities agreed, budget set, exits identified.
- **Annually**: post-season review — squad audit, three-year plan refresh, coach performance review.

### 4.3 Where it breaks

Common failure modes, roughly in order of frequency:

1. **Coach signs players he doesn't use.** Signals the coach was overruled, or that the profile was agreed in the abstract and the coach never really wanted it.
2. **Director signs for the system, not the coach.** Technically correct, practically useless if the coach won't pick the player. A player the coach doesn't trust is a wasted asset regardless of how well he fits the model.
3. **Agent capture.** Either party maintaining a private channel to intermediaries. Corrodes trust fastest of all.
4. **Ambiguous veto.** Both parties believe they had final say on a signing that failed.
5. **Public divergence.** A coach saying "that wasn't my signing" in a press conference is a terminal symptom; the relationship is already over.
6. **The director evaluating the coach he hired.** Structural conflict of interest — the SD who appointed the coach is the least objective judge of whether to sack him. Good boards account for this.

## 5. Reports and artefacts produced

This is where the role becomes concrete. A Sporting Director's output is largely written.

### 5.1 Squad Audit (annual, refreshed mid-season)
The foundational document. Per player: age, contract expiry, wage, book value (amortised), estimated market value, minutes played, role in the system, injury load, and a judgement — *core / rotation / squad filler / saleable / exit*. Aggregated views:

- **Age curve** — headcount by age band per position. Reveals cliffs (four starters all turning 31 together) and gaps (nothing between 22 and 28 in midfield).
- **Contract runway** — a Gantt of expiry dates. Anything inside 18 months is a decision; anything inside 12 is a problem.
- **Positional depth map** — starter / backup / emergency by position, with the system's actual roles rather than generic positions.

### 5.2 Wage Structure Review
Wage bill against turnover and against league peers, distribution across bands, outliers (players paid at a level their contribution doesn't justify), and the effect of any renewal or signing on the ceiling. Where wage inflation gets out of hand, it usually happened one "exception" at a time.

### 5.3 Window Plan (pre-window, per window)
The operating document. Ranked priorities with budget allocated to each, the profile specification for every target, the exits required to fund them, and — importantly — the fallback at each priority. Written before the window opens and deliberately hard to change during it, because the whole purpose is to resist opportunism.

### 5.4 Player Profile / Recruitment Brief
Per target position. Not names: **specifications**. Physical requirements, technical requirements, the specific role in the system, age and contract parameters, cost ceiling, and explicit exclusions. Written by the SD with the coach, and it becomes the scorecard against which every candidate is assessed.

### 5.5 Target Dossier
Per shortlisted player. Data profile against positional benchmarks, scouting reports (minimum three live viewings from different scouts), video, injury history, contract situation, agent and representation, character and background checks, valuation with comparable transactions, and a projected role and minutes in the first eighteen months. Ends with a single recommendation and a walk-away price.

### 5.6 Sale and Exit Analysis
The mirror image, and the one most clubs under-invest in. Which players are at peak value, what the depreciation curve looks like, who has a market, what the replacement cost is, and what the squad looks like without them. Selling well is the harder discipline.

### 5.7 Post-Window Review
Honest retrospective. What was planned versus what happened, what each signing cost against budget, which targets were missed and why, and what the process got wrong. This is the document that makes the function improve over time; it is also the one most often quietly skipped.

### 5.8 Academy Pathway Report
Per prospect: projected ceiling, projected timeline, minutes needed, loan plan, contract status. Aggregated: how many academy minutes the first team actually gave, and whether the pathway is real or decorative.

### 5.9 Loan Report
Every loaned player: minutes, level, performance, development against plan, and a recommendation — recall, extend, sell, integrate.

### 5.10 Board Report (monthly / quarterly)
The translation layer. Squad status, financial position against PSR headroom, window progress, risks, and any decision requiring board approval. Written for people who are not football specialists.

## 6. How the role is measured

Points and trophies are lagging, noisy and mostly attributable to the coach. Better metrics:

- **Net transfer spend against league position** over a rolling three-year window
- **Value creation** — sale proceeds versus purchase cost plus wages, across all outgoings
- **Hit rate** — proportion of signings reaching the minutes and role projected in their dossier
- **Age profile health** — is the squad ageing or renewing
- **Wage-to-turnover ratio** and its direction
- **Academy conversion** — minutes and sale value generated by academy graduates
- **Contract hygiene** — number of first-team players allowed to enter their final year unresolved
- **Continuity** — does the model survive a coaching change intact

The honest version: the role is judged over three years, on whether the club is stronger and more valuable than when the incumbent arrived, and on whether that would still be true if the head coach left tomorrow.

## 7. Archetypes

Different Sporting Directors run genuinely different jobs. Worth naming, because a report written in one voice reads nothing like another.

**The system-builder (Michael Edwards, Liverpool).** Process over instinct. The model — data, committee, defined profiles — is the product. The coach owns tactics absolutely; the director finds players to fit them and never signs a name. Ruthless on sales: Coutinho funded Van Dijk and Alisson. Speaks in value, timing and structure.

**The trader (Monchi at Sevilla; Luis Campos at Monaco and Lille).** The buy-sell cycle is the business model. Enormous scouting reach into underpriced markets, high volume, high churn, and total unsentimentality about selling. Comfortable with a squad that turns over 30% a year.

**The identity-keeper (Max Eberl at Gladbach; Zorc at Dortmund).** The club's way of playing is the asset. Longer tenures, deeper academy integration, coaches selected for fit with an existing style rather than the reverse.

**The eye (Sven Mislintat).** Scouting-led, backing individual talent judgement over systems. Higher variance — spectacular hits and expensive misses.

**The alignment executive (Txiki Begiristain, City).** Where the coach is the club's defining asset, the role becomes execution and support for a single dominant football vision rather than an independent counterweight.

---

## 8. The one-paragraph version

The Sporting Director owns everything about the football operation except what happens between the first whistle and the last. Their objective is a squad that is competitive now, more valuable next year, compliant with the financial rules, and coherent enough to survive the next coaching change. They work with the head coach on a specification-and-sourcing split — the coach defines the need, the director fills it — and the relationship succeeds or fails on whether the decision rights were agreed in writing before the first disagreement. Their output is documentary: a squad audit, a wage review, a window plan, a profile brief and dossier per target, an exit analysis, and an honest post-window retrospective. They are judged over three years, on whether the club got stronger without getting poorer.
